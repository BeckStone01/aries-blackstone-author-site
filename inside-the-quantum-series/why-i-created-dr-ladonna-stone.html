Inside the Quantum Series

Why I Created Dr. Ladonna Stone

By Aries Blackstone

The family moment that became the beginning of the Quantum Series

Dr. Ladonna Stone began with a request from my daughter.

I had written a short story about her sister, Raven, as a superhero. Ladonna read it and said something very simple:

“I want to be a superhero.”

That was the beginning.

There was no Quantum Innovations yet. No enormous corporation. No battles over technology, governments, espionage, political influence, or control of the future.

There was a father who had written one daughter into a superhero story and another daughter who wanted a superhero of her own.

Once Ladonna said it, I had to decide what kind of hero she would be.

I could have given her extraordinary strength. She could have flown. She could have possessed one of the familiar abilities we associate with superheroes.

But that did not feel right for her.

I became interested in another possibility.

What if her greatest ability was her mind?

That question eventually became Dr. Ladonna Stone.

As I continued developing her, intelligence stopped being simply an ability and became a source of conflict.

Being extraordinarily intelligent sounds like an advantage, and of course it is. Ladonna can understand problems other people struggle to comprehend. She can create technologies other people cannot create. She can see possibilities that others miss.

But I did not want intelligence to function like magic.

If Ladonna could walk into every room, immediately understand every problem, and solve everything before anyone else caught up, she would stop being interesting.

So I began asking what her intelligence could not protect her from.

It cannot make another person honest.

It cannot prevent jealousy.

It cannot make ambition disappear.

It cannot guarantee that the people around her will act rationally.

It cannot tell her what to do when two defensible choices lead to very different consequences.

And it cannot remove the fear a mother feels when someone she loves may be in danger.

That is when Ladonna stopped being merely a brilliant fictional scientist and became a character I wanted to keep exploring.

Her intelligence gives her tremendous capability, but capability creates expectations.

If you are able to solve problems other people cannot solve, how long before people begin believing you are responsible for solving them?

If you invent something capable of changing millions of lives, does it still belong only to you?

If your company becomes more technologically capable than some governments, should governments simply leave you alone?

If you know something could help people, are you obligated to use it?

Those questions grew with Ladonna.

So did the world around her.

Quantum Innovations became larger because the consequences of her abilities had to become larger. Governments would eventually notice what she was creating. Competitors would want it. Some people would admire her. Others would resent the authority her abilities gave her.

And some would believe they deserved what she had created.

That human reaction became as important to me as the technology itself.

Representation was also deliberate.

Ladonna is a Black and Thai woman. She occupies spaces associated with science, engineering, national security, enormous wealth, corporate leadership, and technological innovation.

I did not want the books constantly stopping to explain why she deserved to be there.

She belongs there.

Her heritage matters because it is part of who she is, but it is not a justification for her presence in the story.

The narrative does not ask whether a woman who looks like Ladonna can become one of the most capable scientists and business leaders in the world.

It begins from the position that she already has.

Then the story asks what she does with that ability.

That distinction matters to me.

Ladonna is also a wife and mother.

Michael, Hakeem, Aliah, and Jay keep her world from becoming nothing more than corporations, laboratories, government meetings, and technological problems.

She can make decisions involving billions or trillions of dollars and still have to think about what those decisions mean when she goes home.

She can deal with national-security concerns and still be someone's mother.

She can command extraordinary resources and still face situations where power is useless.

Those relationships keep the scale of the Quantum Series connected to something much more ordinary.

Protecting the people you love.

Doing work that matters.

Trying to succeed without allowing success to consume everything else.

Wondering whether the decisions you make for the right reasons will create consequences you never intended.

Those are not superhero problems.

They are human problems.

The Quantum Series simply places them inside extraordinary circumstances.

Looking back, I could never have predicted where Ladonna's request would lead.

A child wanted to be a superhero.

That became a scientist.

The scientist became an inventor.

The inventor became a business leader.

The business leader became someone whose decisions could affect corporations, governments, families, and eventually much more.

But underneath all of it, I still remember where she began.

My daughter read a story about her sister and said:

“I want to be a superhero.”

So I created one for her.

Everything else came after that.
